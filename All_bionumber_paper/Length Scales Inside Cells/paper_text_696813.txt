Screening of membrane surface charges by divalent cations: an atomic representation

Abstract
To arrive at a clear and atomically realistic representation of the process of ionic screening, a model with the following necessary and justifiable constraints was devised. 1) The minimum internuclear distance (IND) between a negative site on the membrane and a cation screening the site is equal to the sum of the site's "equivalent" radius (rs) + the diameter of a water molecule (approximately 2.8 A) + crystal radius of the cation (rc). 2) The average value for the dielectric constant (D) over IND is given by D approximately 80 ((IND - rs - rc)/IND). When this simple atomic model for ionic screening is employed in conjunction with equilibrium ion-selectivity theory, it is possible to predict quantitatively, from coulombic energy calculations, the secondary stereospecific actions of certain alkaline-earth cations as well as the predominant screening effect of these divalent cations at the surfaces of different types of membranes. The model also successfully predicts the transition from a predominantly screening situation to a predominantly binding situation, which was observed experimentally when negative surface charge density was decreased in nerve.

PHYSIOLOGICAL LITERATURE containS numerouS example s of cation screening at the charged surfaces of both biological membranes and artificial lipid bilayers (10, 31,41,44).Past theoretical treatments of ionic screening(basedon diffuse double-layer theory)have incorporated assumptions that are oversimplified at the chemical level into their sophisticated mathematical logic.As a result,no readily visualized,reasonably complete,mechanistic description has yet been for mulated for the molecular events involvedin the“screening" process, that is, the process by which the surface potential of a charged membrane interface is altered to the same extent by all ions of the same valence. One fundamental question needs to be answered at an atomic level. What physical picture will explain how different ions(of the same valence)in solution can have virtually the same interaction(or screening)energies with the membrane charge sites, despite very definite differences in the atomic radii and hydration energies oftheions?Furthermore,how do the screen in gene rgies compare and interrelate with the corresponding binding energies for the individual ions?Thisstudy provides a workable atomic model that answers the above questions for the case with the greatest biological significance-divalent cation screening.Anabstract (13）andabrief report(15）describing someofthe results have been published.

The Goldschmidt radii were derived from oxide crystals and are,therefore,the most appropri ate when dealing with biological membranes and phospholipid bilayer s(as evidenced by equilibrium ionselectivity calculations (18)).  

However,the ionic radius of a given alkaline-earth cation is not the only limitation on how closely the cation can approach the membrane surface in a screen- ing situation.The thickness of the"hydration shell" around the cation must also be considered.(Sucha consideration has helped give rise to the non specific concept of the outer Helmholtz plane in diffuse doublelayer theory(1).)This consideration is necessary simply because in a pure screening situation no bound water molecules are stripped from the counterion, i.e., the hydration energy has no relevance,(1o).Because only the first layer of water molecules around a monatomic cation has energies of interaction with the cation that are large compared with the thermal energy(38), the thickness of the hydration shell around an alkalineearth cation is approximately equal to the diameter of a water molecule  $(\approx\!2.8$  A).(This result can differ markedly from that obtained when the so-called hyd rated radius of a counter ion，derived from mobility measurements (38),is used(cf.Ref.8).)Consequently it would be expected that in a screening situation the minimum distance between the alkaline earth'scenter of charge and the membrane surface is approximately equal to the sum of the cation's ionic radius  $^+$  the diameter of a water molecule.  

One needs toconsider what ismeant bythemembrane surface."Is the local surface actually that of an an ionic site on the membrane or that of a water molecule bound to the an ionic site?Does one need to consider the hydration energy of the surface anionic site on the membrane? The preferred orientation of a water molecule adjacent to a membrane an ionic site (i.e., hydrogen atoms oriented toward the membrane site (3,38))should bethe same as that of an intervening water molecule in the hydration shell of an approaching,oppositely-charged counterion (i.e.,hydrogen atom s oriented away from the cation and toward the membrane site(3,38)for divalent cations the size of magnesium or 1 a rger(39)).Hence,the orientation of the intervening water molecule bound to the an ionic site(and likewise the corresponding ion-permanentdipole and ion-quadrupole interactions between the site and water molecule) would not be altered significantly by the presence of a divalent cation next to the oxygen end of this water molecule (cf. Ref. 27). The rest of the water molecules in the site's hydration shell should not experience an appreciable change in orientation,because they would be separated from the cation and largely shielded from its electrostatic field(38,42)by thecation's hydration shell.Because no significant change in water orientation adjacent to the an ionic site (and likewise no appreciable change in the aforementioned electrostatic interactions) is predicted,it can be assumed that a negligible free-energy change (cf. Ref. 40)would be necessary for a membrane site to simply trade one (or more) of its waters of hydration for a portion of the alkaline earth's hydration shell; that is, one would expect the hydration energy of the bare site to be approximately equal to the hydration energy of the site during sharing of the cation's hydration shell. Therefore, in this analysis of divalent cation screening, the hydration shell of a membrane an ionic site is not considered to be a limiting factor on how closely the cation can approach the ionic surface of that site.  

The molecular configuration of the fixed anionic sites on the membrane surface is the next major consideration.According to diffuse double-layer theory,thisis meaningless because the charge on the membrane is assumed to be uniformly smeared over the membrane surface (25,36).An alternate model assuming the membrane charges to be discrete ly localized may lead to different predictions concerning the relations among membrane potential profile,average inter charge spacing.and the nature of the ionic solution per fusing the membrane. This fact has already been pointed out (6) and discussed (4). Furthermore, unless one considers the more realistic situation of localized charge sites on the membrane,coulombic energy calculations of the type so successfully used in equilibrium ion-selectivity theory(18,20,21)would not be possible.Conceivably, such energy calculations could afford a clear atomic understanding both of  $\pmb{I}$  )the screening process(comparable to the understanding already achieved with equilibrium ion selectivity)，and  $\mathcal{Z}$  )the interplay of binding with screening observed at numerous artificial bilayers (31) and biological membranes (11, 30).  

Unfortunately,our knowledge is uncertain regarding the exact structure of the an ionic groups(probably phosphate and carboxyl groups (9, 12, 14, 18, 20)) at biological membrane surfaces.A similar uncertainty exists concerning the precise stereochemical and ele ctronic configuration ofknown anionic groupsat artificial membrane surfaces.However,these difficulties have been successfully circumvented in the past and useful energy calculations have been performed by considering only the equivalent field strength of a given membrane an ionic site.The field strength of a site can be effectively expressed as the “equivalent" radius of an energetically equivalent singly charged anion(18,20,21);the smaller the equivalent radius of an an ionic site,the higher is its field strength.However,the term equivalent radius,as used in this paper, is not meant to represent a truelinear dimension;the equivalent radius is simply a measure of the actual field strength of an anionic site with exact ionic radius and charge magnitude unknown.This convenient and effective physical representation of a membrane anionic site can now be incorporated into anatomic representation of the divalent cation screening process. # DISCUSSION  

McLaughlin et al.(31)have calculated,from carrier- mediated conductance data, a  $\pmb{\sigma}_{\mathrm{initial}}$  of  $\mathbf{1\,e^{\dot{-}/\mathbf{38\,\AA^{2}}}}$  (at  $\mathbf{pH}$  7.2）for both cholesterol-free ph os phat idyls erin e(of animal origin)and ph os phat idyl glycerol bilayer membranes.The surface potential arising from these surface charges was shown to be altered substantially by the presence of even low concentrations of various alkaline-earth cations  $(\mathbf{Mg^{2+}}$  ，  $\mathbf{C}\mathbf{a}^{2+}$  ，  $\mathrm{\bfS r^{2+}}$  ，  $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{a}^{2+}$  ）in the bathing solution.Most of the alterations in surface potential could be explained in terms of a simple screening process.  

As pointed out previously (1l)，a pure screening process is one in which the ions in solution do not approach closely enough to the oppositely charged groups on the membrane surface to give any imp ortance to such factors as the ion's radius, polar iz ability, or hydration energy.Within experimental resolution, the only factors that determine the concentration of ions in the immediate vicinity of the membrane surface are the density of surface charges on the membrane and the valence of the ions in solution.Hence,inthis atomic model for divalent cation screening,at a given surface charge density one would expect the calculated totalfree energies of screening  $(\Delta\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{screen~T}})$  for the different alkaline earths to differ in appreciably from one another (cf. INTRODUCTIoN). The range of  $\Delta\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{screen~T}}$  values calculated for the four alkaline earths at any given immediate-nei oh hor site non nl ation(where  $\pmb{\sigma}_{\mathrm{initial}}$   $\mathbf{\Sigma=1\ e^{-}/38\ \AA^{2}})$   never exceeds 0.76 kcal/mol and, more often,the range falls considerably below this value (Tables 1-4). This maximum range of  $\mathbf{0.76\kcal/mol}$  is not much different from thermal energy(  $.R T\:\approx\:0.6$  kcal/mol at room temperature), so that the calculated Fscreen T values fulfill the theoretical expectation.  

In their experiments with negatively charged bilayers, McLaughlin et al. (31) also noted secondary stereospecific actions of certain alkaline-earth cations along with the predominant screening effect of these divalents.Such secondary binding effects ofalkaline earths, particularly calcium,have also been demonstrated with various biological membranes (22). However,with one exception(seebelow),these studies have not examined in detail the separate effects of all four common alkaline earths. This limitation makes analy. sis in terms of equilibrium ion-selectivity theory diff icult and possibly misleading, so that this atomic model has been constructed from the more complete artificialbilayer data. Nonetheless, as will be evidenced below, the atomic interactions on which the screening model is based are applicable to both artificial bilayers and biological membranes. The secondary binding effects noted by McLaughlin et al. differed with the type of phospholipid used to form the artificial bilayer.  

With ph os phat idyl glycerol bilayers, it was found that,of the four common alkaline earths,only calcium had a significantly greater effect on the surface po tentialthan that predicted by simple screening theory (Gouy-Chapman theory). It was concluded that calcium bound to negative sites onthe ph os phat idyl glycerol membrane with an association constant of approximately 0.05 liter/mol (Ref. 31, Fig. 4). However, when ph os phat idyls erin e bilayer s were used,  $\mathrm{{\bfS}r^{2+}}$  and  $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{a}^{2+}$  only screened the membrane charge sites,whereas  $\mathbf{M}\bar{\mathbf{g^{2+}}}$  and  $\mathbf{C}\mathbf{a}^{2+}$  appeared to bind as well as screen the sites.The association constants of both  $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{g}^{2+}$  and  $\mathbf{C}\mathbf{a}^{2+}$  for the sites on the ph os phat idyls erin e membrane were found to be approximately 0.1 liter/mol (Ref. 31, Fig. 3).  

The anionic sites on the ph os phat idyl glycerol and ph os phat idyls erin e bilayer s can be conveniently and effectively represented with the physically valid conce pt of equivalent site radius(for a singly charged anion)(THEory).The value chosenfor the equivalent radius is moderately important for calculation of physic ally reasonable values of screening energies  $(\Delta\mathbf{F}_{s c r e e n\ \mathbf{T}})$  ，but it becomes extremely critical (and hence, more meaningful physically) when attempting to quantitatively account for secondary stereospecific effects that accompany screening (i.e., in comparison of  $\Delta\mathbf{F}_{s c r e e n\textit{T}}$  and  $\Delta\mathbf{F_{bind\,T}}$  of a given divalent cation). With this atomic model, at a fixed  $\pmb{\sigma}_{\mathrm{initial}}$  , there is only one precise value for the equivalent site radius that will correctly predict a given secondary binding situation. This is to be expected in the physical situation involving a completely homogeneous population of anionic sites on a given membrane, as assumed in the present atomic model. The resulting success in quantitative explanation of the bilayer binding data(seebelow)may be due to one of the following: 1) only one species of anionic site is not sterically hindered from being screened and bound by alkaline-earth cations, or 2) the equivalent site radius effectively represents the equiv. a lent field strength of the entire(net negatively charged)polar complex of the particular phospholipid used toform thebilayer.The second alternative might also explain why the calculations of alkaline-earth association constants are so sensitive (REsuLrs) to the value chosen for the equivalent site radius:theequivalent radius is a simplified, yet highly effective (cf. Refs. 18, 20, 21), sum mari z ation of several important physical attributes of a membrane site that contribute to its actual field strength in different molecular en v ironments.These attributes include the stereochemical configuration, electron distribution, and ionic radii of the constituent atoms in the site. The more complex a membrane anionic site,the more minute can be the variations in the resultant field strength of the site, i.e.,the equivalent site radius.

All the above theoretical association constants have been calculated for the situation in which all the immediate-neighbor sites surrounding the central binding site are exposed (non neutralized). Obviously, this situation would notexistif two neighboring sites on the membrane were simultaneously bound by tw c cations.Because the known,very small association constants of the alkaline earths for the membrane sites treated in this study render such a possibility sufficiently improbable (from kinetic considerations (cf Ref. 14)) it has not been considered in the above calculations.On the other hand,this possibility becomes much more probable when one of the two bound cations is a hydrogen ion.This is simply because the association constant of  $\mathbf{H}^{+}$  for the membrane site is very large, so that its “average occupancy time"(cf. Ref.14)at the site is relatively long.Thehigher the hydrogen ion concentration(i.e.,the lower the  $\mathbf{pH})$  ，the higher is the number of immediate-neighbor sites, surrounding a central binding site,that is likely to be neutralized. Therefore, with a central binding site as a vantage point, a substantial decrease in surface charge density (from a decrease in  $\mathbf{pH})$  will be reflected by a decreasein the number of non neutralized,immediateneighbor sites surrounding the central site. Number in this case refers to an average value over time.
